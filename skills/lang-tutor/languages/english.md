# English Tutor Guide

Read this together with `_common.md`, which defines the feedback block formats, the universal deep-dive types, and the baseline level tables. This file supplies what is specific to English.

## Conventions

Default to General American spelling and usage; note British/Australian/Canadian variants (colour vs. color, "have you got" vs. "do you have") only when relevant or when the user prefers them.

Unlike other guides in this skill, the learner's native language varies widely (Spanish, Chinese, Arabic, Portuguese, etc.) — do not assume a specific native-language contrast unless the user's messages reveal one (e.g. dropped articles suggests a native language without articles, like Russian or Chinese; consistent subject-verb order errors suggest an SOV native language, like Japanese or Korean). Infer the likely native-language interference pattern from the errors you actually see, and tailor explanations accordingly.

## Framework Alignment: CEFR

The three proficiency levels map onto the Common European Framework of Reference — the standard behind Cambridge English, IELTS, and most ESL curricula:

| Guide level | CEFR bands | Can-do target |
|---|---|---|
| Beginner | A1-A2 | Make simple statements about self, routine, and immediate needs; handle short predictable exchanges |
| Intermediate | B1-B2 | Sustain conversation on familiar and some abstract topics; handle most travel, work, and study situations; express and support opinions |
| Advanced | C1-C2 | Near-native fluency; handles abstract, implicit, and nuanced meaning; flexible, precise register control |

## Grammar Syllabus

The real CEFR teaching sequence. Within each level, earlier items are foundational and should be taught before later ones. This drives both the Grammar Corrections priority in Mode 1 and the Concept Spotlight in Mode 2.

- **Beginner (A1-A2)**: be/have → present simple → articles a/an/the → plurals & countability → past simple → present continuous for future plans → comparatives/superlatives → "going to" future
- **Intermediate (B1-B2)**: present perfect (simple & continuous) vs. simple past → past continuous → first conditional → second conditional → modals of deduction (must/might/can't) & obligation (should/have to) → passive voice → reported speech → relative clauses (defining/non-defining)
- **Advanced (C1-C2)**: mixed conditionals → inversion for emphasis ("Rarely have I seen...") → advanced hedging/modality (would argue, tends to) → cohesive/discourse markers (nonetheless, whereby) → nominalization for formal register → subtle tense/aspect distinctions (future perfect continuous, etc.)

Add this row to both level tables in `_common.md`:

| Aspect | Beginner (A1-A2) | Intermediate (B1-B2) | Advanced (C1-C2) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grammar focus (CEFR) | be/have, present simple, articles, plurals, past simple, present continuous for future, comparatives | present perfect, past continuous, conditionals 1-2, modals, passive, reported speech, relative clauses | mixed conditionals, inversion, hedging, discourse markers, nominalization |

## Error Categories to Watch For

In Mode 1 Grammar Corrections, prioritize: article use (a/an/the/zero article), preposition choice (in/on/at, dependent prepositions like "depend on," "good at"), subject-verb agreement, present perfect vs. simple past ("I have seen" vs. "I saw"), countable vs. uncountable nouns ("advices" → "advice"), word order (adverb placement, question inversion), phrasal verb particles.

In Idiom & Naturalness Suggestions, prefer phrasal verbs and common collocations over stiff formal equivalents when the register calls for it (e.g. "figure out" over "ascertain" in casual contexts).

In Vocabulary Translations, flag false friends when the user's native language is identifiable from context (e.g. Spanish *actualmente* → "currently," not "actually"; German *bekommen* → "to get," not "to become").

## Irregulars to Prioritize

Feeds the Irregularity Watch in `_common.md`. English has little inflection but what it has is heavily irregular, and the irregular items are the highest-frequency words in the language — so they cannot be deferred.

**Class irregular** — teach the class, not the word:
- **Vowel-gradation verbs** fall into real families: *sing/sang/sung, ring/rang/rung, swim/swam/swum* · *drive/drove/driven, write/wrote/written, ride/rode/ridden* · *teach/taught, catch/caught, buy/bought, bring/brought*. Naming the family turns 180 memorizations into a dozen patterns.
- **No-change verbs**: *put, cut, let, set, hit, cost, hurt* — identical in all three parts. Learners over-correct these into *putted*; reassure that the bare form is right.
- **Foreign-origin plurals**: *criterion → criteria*, *phenomenon → phenomena*, *analysis → analyses*, *index → indices*. Note that *data* and *media* are contested in modern usage — don't correct these flatly.

**Locally irregular** — name where it's safe:
- Irregular verbs are irregular **only in the past and participle**. The present tense, the -ing form, and the third-person -s are perfectly regular for every verb except *be* and *have*. Say this — learners often assume an irregular verb is unpredictable everywhere.
- Irregularity is **almost entirely confined to the 200-odd most common verbs**. Any verb the learner coins or borrows will be regular. This is genuinely reassuring.

**Fully irregular** — must be memorized:
- ***be*** — the only verb in English with distinct forms across person *and* number (*am/is/are/was/were/been*), and the most irregular word in the language
- *go → went* (suppletive — *went* was originally the past of *wend*)
- Irregular plurals: *children, feet, teeth, mice, geese, men, women, oxen*; and unchanged plurals *sheep, deer, fish, series, species*
- Irregular comparatives: *good → better → best*, *bad → worse → worst*, *far → further/farther*, *little → less → least*

**By level**: at A1-A2 prioritize *be, have, go, do* and the top-50 irregular past forms; at B1-B2 the participle forms needed for present perfect and passive, plus irregular plurals; at C1-C2 the contested cases (*data*, *criteria*, *further vs. farther*) as register choices rather than errors.

## Additional Deep-Dive Types

These join the rotation alongside Concept Spotlight and Word Family from `_common.md`.

- **Phrasal Verb Family**: Pick one phrasal verb from the Key Vocabulary and show 2-3 related forms with the same base verb and different particles (e.g. *look up*, *look into*, *look after*), noting how the particle shifts the meaning.

- **Register & Formality Ladder**: Pick one phrase from the translation and show it at 2-3 formality levels (casual → neutral → formal/academic), with a one-line note on when each is appropriate. CEFR treats pragmatic register control as a defining marker of B2+ competence, so favor this for intermediate-advanced users (e.g. *"get info on"* → *"find out about"* → *"obtain information regarding"*).

For the English **Word Family** deep-dive specifically, favor noun/verb/adjective/adverb derivations (*decide → decision → decisive → decisively*) with a note on stress shift or spelling change.

## Watch-outs

- **Articles**: the single most persistent error for learners whose native language has no article system (Russian, Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Hindi) — correct every miss at beginner level with a one-line rule (specific/known → "the"; general/first mention → "a/an" or zero article)
- **Present perfect vs. simple past**: many languages collapse this distinction into one past tense — flag with the "connects to now" vs. "finished, detached from now" framing
- **Preposition collocations**: "depend on," "good at," "interested in," "married to" — these don't translate literally from most languages; correct as a fixed pair, not a standalone word choice
- **Uncountable nouns**: "information," "advice," "furniture," "news" take no plural and no "a/an" — a frequent error regardless of native language
- **Phrasal verb literalism**: learners often use the formal Latinate equivalent ("investigate" instead of "look into") because it translates more directly from Romance-language vocabulary — nudge toward the phrasal verb when the register is casual
- **Gerund vs. infinitive after verbs**: "enjoy doing," "avoid doing," "suggest doing" vs. "want to do," "decide to do," "plan to do" — the choice is largely non-rule-governed and must be corrected as fixed verb patterns, not derived from logic
- **Relative clause reduction**: learners often keep the relative pronoun where a native speaker would drop it ("the book that I bought" → "the book I bought") or over-apply reduction where it's ungrammatical (subject relative clauses can't be reduced: "the man who called" ≠ "the man called")
- **False friends beyond the obvious pair**: watch for *sensible* (Spanish/French: sensitive), *eventually* (many languages: possibly, not "in the end"), *embarrassed* vs. *embarazada* (Spanish: pregnant) — flag these immediately since the error is invisible to the learner
- **Subject-verb agreement with distractor nouns**: "the data is/are," "everyone has," "neither of them is" — agreement across an intervening noun ("the list of items *are*...") is a common slip even at higher levels
