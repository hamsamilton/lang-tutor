# Dutch Tutor Guide

Read this together with `_common.md`, which defines the feedback block formats, the universal deep-dive types, and the baseline level tables. This file supplies what is specific to Dutch.

## Conventions

Default to standard Netherlands Dutch; note Flemish (Belgian) differences when relevant. English speakers find Dutch vocabulary friendly but the word order and de/het system treacherous — focus there. Always give nouns with their article.

## Framework Alignment: CEFR / NT2 / CNaVT

Dutch is CEFR-aligned through the Staatsexamen NT2 and the CNaVT certificates, with a distinctive practical wrinkle — the two NT2 programmes are the gatekeepers for work and study in the Netherlands:

| Guide level | CEFR | Exam | Can-do target |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beginner | A1-A2 | Inburgeringsexamen (A2) | de/het and adjective -e; V2 word order; perfect tense; daily transactions |
| Intermediate | B1-B2 | **NT2 Programma I** (B1), **Programma II** (B2) | Subclause word order; *er* constructions; passive; workplace and study fluency |
| Advanced | C1-C2 | CNaVT PAT / educatief professioneel | Modal particles; register range; academic and professional Dutch |

NT2 Programma II (B2) is the standard requirement for Dutch university admission — a useful anchor if the user has a concrete goal.

## Grammar Syllabus

The standard CEFR/NT2 sequence. Within each level, earlier items are foundational and should be taught before later ones. This drives both the Grammar Corrections priority in Mode 1 and the Concept Spotlight in Mode 2.

- **Beginner (A1-A2)**: **de/het gender** → present tense → **verb-second (V2) word order** and inversion in questions → plural -en vs. -s → the adjective **-e** rule and its *een* exception → modal verbs → separable verbs with the prefix at the end → perfect tense with hebben vs. zijn → **'t kofschip** rule for weak past endings
- **Intermediate (B1-B2)**: **subclause verb-final order** (omdat, dat, als, terwijl) → relative clauses (die/dat) → imperfect vs. perfect in narrative → passive with worden vs. zijn → **er in its five distinct uses** (existential, pronominal, locative, quantitative, prepositional) → conditional with zou → middle-field word order (time-manner-place) → comparatives
- **Advanced (C1-C2)**: **modal particles** (toch, maar, even, wel, nou, eens) → formal vs. informal register and the je/u boundary → complex subordination and verb clusters at clause end → Netherlands vs. Flemish usage → idiom and nominal style

## Irregulars to Prioritize

Feeds the Irregularity Watch in `_common.md`. Dutch shares the Germanic strong-verb system with English and German, so English speakers often already know the pattern without realizing it — point at the cognate and the class stops looking foreign.

**Class irregular** — teach the class, not the word:
- **Strong verbs by ablaut class**, the same inherited series as English:

  | Pattern | Infinitive → imperfect → participle | English cognate | Also |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | ij – ee – e | schrijven → schreef → geschreven | write/wrote/written | blijven, kijken, rijden |
  | i – o – o | zingen → zong → gezongen | sing/sang/sung | drinken, vinden, beginnen |
  | e – a – o | breken → brak → gebroken | break/broke/broken | spreken, nemen, stelen |
  | o – ie – o | lopen → liep → gelopen | — | roepen, slapen, laten |

- **'t kofschip** — the one rule every Dutch course teaches, and a genuine gift: a weak verb takes **-te/-t** if its stem ends in one of the consonants in *'t kofschip* (t, k, f, s, ch, p), otherwise **-de/-d**. Werken → werkte; horen → hoorde. Name the rule rather than the form.
- **Spelling changes for vowel length**: an open long vowel doubles when the syllable closes (maken → maak, ik maak), and a consonant doubles to keep a vowel short (man → mannen, zitten → zit). Purely orthographic, so reassure.
- **Gender by suffix** is partly predictable: **-ing, -heid, -teit, -tie, -ie** → *de*; the diminutive **-je** and **-sel, -isme** → *het*. Give the rule whenever a matching noun appears.
- **Plural -s vs. -en**: words ending in an unstressed syllable (-el, -em, -en, -er, -je) take -s; most others take -en. A rule, not a coin flip.

**Locally irregular** — name where it's safe:
- Dutch verbs conjugate for **only three distinct present-tense forms** (ik / jij-u-hij / wij-jullie-zij) — far simpler than German. Say so.
- Strong verbs are irregular only in the imperfect and participle; the present tense is regular for every verb except *zijn, hebben, kunnen, zullen, willen, mogen*.

**Fully irregular** — must be memorized:
- **zijn** (suppletive: ben / was / geweest), plus *hebben*, and the modals *kunnen, zullen, willen, mogen, moeten* — all irregular in the present singular
- **de/het gender has no reliable rule** beyond the suffix list above. Roughly two-thirds of nouns are *de*, but guessing produces the adjective error downstream. Learn every noun with its article — this is the single most under-taught irregularity in Dutch.
- The **adjective -e exception**: het-words take **no** -e after *een* — *een groot huis* but *het grote huis*, against *een grote man*. Learners get gender right and still miss this.
- Irregular comparatives: *goed → beter → best*, *veel → meer → meest*, *weinig → minder → minst*, *graag → liever → liefst*

**By level**: at A1-A2 prioritize *zijn/hebben*, the modals, 't kofschip, and the article of every new noun; at B1-B2 the strong-verb classes and the *een* adjective exception; at C1-C2 irregular participles in verb clusters.

## Error Categories to Watch For

In Mode 1 Grammar Corrections, prioritize: de/het gender and the adjective -e ending it controls, verb-second word order in main clauses and verb-final in subclauses (*omdat ik het leuk vind*), separable verbs (prefix to the end: *ik bel je op*), word order in the perfect tense (*hij heeft het boek gelezen*), and *er* constructions.

Watch for literal English transfers that are grammatical but un-Dutch.

In Vocabulary Translations, always give the article, and flag false friends proactively: *eventueel* (possibly, not eventually), *brutaal* (cheeky), *slim* (smart), *winkel* (shop), *monster* (sample), *zakelijk* (businesslike).

## Additional Deep-Dive Types

These join the rotation alongside Concept Spotlight and Word Family from `_common.md`.

- **Conjugation Table**: Use the table format and level scaling defined in `_common.md`, with these Dutch specifics:
  - **Persons**: Dutch collapses to **three distinct present forms** — use rows ik / jij-u-hij-zij-het / wij-jullie-zij rather than six. Note the *jij* inversion quirk: *jij werkt* but *werk jij?* (the -t drops).
  - **Beginner (A1-A2)**: present tense, then the imperfect showing 't kofschip for weak verbs or the ablaut for strong ones
  - **Intermediate (B1-B2)**: present / imperfect / perfect side by side — the three principal parts, with hebben vs. zijn marked
  - **Advanced (C1-C2)**: verb clusters at clause end, showing how the participle and infinitive order
  - **Pattern line**: name the class (weak with -te or -de, or strong with its ablaut pattern) and one more verb in it

- **Word Order X-ray**: Take the translated sentence (or one clause) and mark the verb positions — what sits in second position, what gets kicked to the end, and why. Show the same clause as main clause vs. subclause if instructive. This is the highest-value Dutch deep-dive; favor it whenever the sentence has a subclause.

- **Word Family / Compounds**: Pick one word from the Key Vocabulary and show 2-3 relatives — compounds or derivations (e.g. werk: werken, werkgever, werknemer), noting how Dutch compounds glue together where English separates.

## Watch-outs

- **De/het**: there's no reliable rule — teach every noun with its article, and note that het-words take no adjective -e after *een* (*een groot huis*)
- **Subclause word order**: omdat/dat/als send the verb to the end — the top structural error; flag every miss with the conjunction that caused it
- **Separable verbs**: opbellen → *ik bel je op* — prefix to the end in main clauses, reattached in subclauses
- **False friends**: *eventueel*, *brutaal*, *zakelijk*, *monster* — call out proactively
- **Going too English**: Dutch tolerates many anglicisms, but flag literal transfers a native wouldn't use — *"dat maakt zin"* is not Dutch (it's *dat is logisch* / *dat slaat ergens op*)
- **'t kofschip**: when a weak past tense is wrong, give the rule rather than the form — the learner will hit the same choice on the next verb
- **Er**: learners either omit *er* entirely or overuse it; when correcting, name which of its five functions is at play
