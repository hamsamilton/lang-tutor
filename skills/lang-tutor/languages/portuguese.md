# Portuguese Tutor Guide

Read this together with `_common.md`, which defines the feedback block formats, the universal deep-dive types, and the baseline level tables. This file supplies what is specific to Portuguese.

## Conventions

Default to Brazilian Portuguese; flag European Portuguese differences (tu conjugation, estar a + infinitive, object pronoun placement, vocabulary) when they're significant or when the user prefers European.

## Framework Alignment: CEFR / CAPLE / CELPE-Bras

Portuguese has **two** competing certification systems, split by variety — worth knowing which one a learner is aiming at:

- **CAPLE** (Portugal) is CEFR-aligned one-to-one: CIPLE (A2), DEPLE (B1), DIPLE (B2), DAPLE (C1), DUPLE (C2).
- **CELPE-Bras** (Brazil) is the only Brazilian-government certificate. It uses its own four bands — Intermediário, Intermediário Superior, Avançado, Avançado Superior (roughly B1 through C2) — and notably **does not certify beginners at all**.

Map to CEFR as the common denominator:

| Guide level | CEFR | CAPLE | CELPE-Bras | Can-do target |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Beginner | A1-A2 | CIPLE | *(not certified)* | Describe self, family, and routine; handle predictable transactions and simple past narration |
| Intermediate | B1-B2 | DEPLE, DIPLE | Intermediário, Interm. Superior | Narrate and argue in past and future; express wishes and doubt; handle work and study topics fluently |
| Advanced | C1-C2 | DAPLE, DUPLE | Avançado, Avançado Superior | Full mood control including personal infinitive; nuanced register; comfortable across both varieties |

## Grammar Syllabus

Standard CEFR-aligned Portuguese sequencing. Within each level, earlier items are foundational and should be taught before later ones. This drives both the Grammar Corrections priority in Mode 1 and the Concept Spotlight in Mode 2.

- **Beginner (A1-A2)**: gender & articles → **contractions** (de+o → do, em+a → na, por+o → pelo, em+um → num) → present indicative → ser vs. estar vs. ficar → plurals including the -ão family (-ões / -ãos / -ães) → ir + infinitive future → pretérito perfeito → imperfeito
- **Intermediate (B1-B2)**: pretérito perfeito vs. imperfeito → present subjunctive + its triggers (quero que, talvez, embora) → **future subjunctive** (quando/se/enquanto + subj.) → object pronoun placement (Brazilian proclisis vs. European enclisis) → pretérito perfeito composto (*tenho feito* = repeated/ongoing, **not** a simple present perfect) → conditional → imperfect subjunctive and si-clauses
- **Advanced (C1-C2)**: **personal infinitive** (inflected infinitive) in complex clauses → sequence of tenses across moods → pluperfect simple (*fizera*) as a literary form → discourse markers (todavia, por conseguinte) → register and Brazilian vs. European syntactic divergence → literary constructions

Add this row to both level tables in `_common.md`:

| Aspect | Beginner (A1-A2) | Intermediate (B1-B2) | Advanced (C1-C2) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grammar focus (CEFR) | gender/articles, contractions, present indicative, ser/estar/ficar, -ão plurals, perfeito, imperfeito | perfeito vs. imperfeito, present & future subjunctive, pronoun placement, perfeito composto, conditional | personal infinitive, sequence of tenses, pluperfect simple, discourse markers, variety divergence |

## Two Features With No English (or Spanish) Equivalent

These deserve extra patience and should be named explicitly whenever they come up — learners coming from English or Spanish do not have them and will not produce them unprompted.

- **Future subjunctive**: living and obligatory in Portuguese after *quando*, *se*, *enquanto*, *assim que* when referring to future time — *quando eu **tiver** tempo* ("when I have time"). Spanish lost this form; English uses a plain present. Expect learners to write *quando eu tenho tempo* and correct it as a structural rule, not a slip.
- **Personal infinitive**: an infinitive that conjugates for person — *é importante **eles chegarem** cedo* ("it's important that they arrive early"). Unique to Portuguese among major Romance languages. Introduce it as an alternative to a subjunctive clause when subjects differ.

## Error Categories to Watch For

In Mode 1 Grammar Corrections, prioritize: ser vs. estar vs. ficar, missing contractions (do, na, pelo, num), gender/number agreement, preterite vs. imperfect, missed subjunctive triggers (quero que, talvez, embora), missed future subjunctive after quando/se.

In Vocabulary Translations, flag false friends proactively: *pasta* (folder/briefcase), *puxar* (to pull, despite looking like push), *pretender* (to intend), *atualmente* (currently), *esquisito* (weird, not exquisite), *novela* (soap opera).

## Irregulars to Prioritize

Feeds the Irregularity Watch in `_common.md`.

**Class irregular** — teach the class, not the word:
- **Stem-changers**: e→i (*servir, preferir*), o→u (*dormir, cobrir*) in the 1sg present; plus the vowel-quality alternations of *poder, fazer, trazer*
- **Spelling-change verbs**: -car → -qu-, -gar → -gu-, -çar → -c- before *e* (*ficar → fiquei*); and -ger/-gir → -j- before *a/o* (*proteger → protejo*)
- **The -ão plural family**: *-ões* (most: *coração → corações*), *-ãos* (*mão → mãos*), *-ães* (*pão → pães*). Three outcomes from one ending — flag every time, since there is no reliable rule.

**Locally irregular** — name where it's safe:
- **The imperfeito is almost entirely regular** — only *ser (era)*, *ter (tinha)*, *vir (vinha)*, *pôr (punha)* are irregular. Worth saying explicitly.
- **The future subjunctive is derived, not memorized**: take the 3pl preterite, drop *-am* — *fizeram → fizer*, *tiveram → tiver*, *forem*. So a verb irregular in the preterite is *predictably* irregular in the future subjunctive. This turns the language's most intimidating form into a mechanical step; say so whenever the future subjunctive appears.

**Fully irregular** — must be memorized:
- *ser*, *estar*, *ir*, *ter*, *haver*, *pôr* (the only -or verb; its whole family *compor, dispor, supor* follows it)
- The shared preterite **fui/foi** serving *both* ser and ir, exactly as in Spanish
- Irregular past participles: *feito, dito, visto, posto, escrito, aberto, vindo*
- **Double participles** — a Portuguese specialty: many verbs have both a regular form used with *ter/haver* and an irregular one used with *ser/estar* — *tinha aceitado* but *foi aceito*; likewise *entregado/entregue*, *matado/morto*, *pagado/pago*. Flag this whenever such a verb appears; learners never guess it.
- Irregular comparatives: *bom → melhor*, *mau → pior*, *grande → maior*

**By level**: at A1-A2 prioritize *ser/estar/ter/ir/fazer* and the -ão plurals; at B1-B2 the preterite irregulars (which then unlock the future subjunctive) and double participles; at C1-C2 *pôr* and its family, and the pluperfect simple.

## Additional Deep-Dive Types

These join the rotation alongside Concept Spotlight and Word Family from `_common.md`.

- **Contraction Corner**: If the translation contains a contraction (do, na, pelo, num...), unpack it into its parts and show the pattern with one more example.

- **Conjugation Table**: Use the table format and level scaling defined in `_common.md`, with these Portuguese specifics:
  - **Persons**: for Brazilian Portuguese use the 4-form spoken paradigm (eu / você-ele-ela / nós *or* a gente / vocês-eles-elas) — *tu* is regional in Brazil and its verb form is often not conjugated in speech. For European Portuguese use the full 6 with *tu* and *vós* omitted only if archaic.
  - **Beginner (A1-A2)**: present indicative first; then pretérito perfeito, then imperfeito
  - **Intermediate (B1-B2)**: perfeito vs. imperfeito side by side; then **present subjunctive vs. future subjunctive** — the future subjunctive table is the highest-value one in Portuguese, since the form is unfamiliar to English and Spanish speakers alike
  - **Advanced (C1-C2)**: the **personal infinitive** alongside the present subjunctive, showing where each is used with a differing subject; or sequence of tenses across moods
  - **Pattern line**: name whether the verb is regular (-ar/-er/-ir) or irregular, and give one verb from the same class. For the future subjunctive, note that it is built from the 3rd-person plural preterite stem (*fizeram* → *fizer*), which makes irregular verbs predictable.

- **Brazilian vs. European Contrast**: Take one phrase from the translation and show it in both varieties, noting the specific divergence (gerund *estou fazendo* vs. *estou a fazer*; proclisis *me diga* vs. enclisis *diga-me*; *você* vs. *tu*; vocabulary such as *ônibus*/*autocarro*). Favor this at intermediate-advanced, where handling both varieties becomes a real competence.

For the Portuguese **Word Family** deep-dive specifically, favor verb/noun/adjective derivations (*trabalhar → trabalho → trabalhador*) or English cognate chains with their sound-correspondence rule (-ção → -tion, -dade → -ty).

## Watch-outs

- **Ser vs. estar vs. ficar**: the three-way split trips Spanish learners and English speakers alike — give the one-line reason on every correction
- **Missing contractions**: *de o* / *em a* instead of *do* / *na* is the most visible beginner tell — correct it every time
- **Future subjunctive omission**: *quando eu tenho tempo* → *quando eu tiver tempo* — the single most reliable non-native marker at intermediate level
- **False friends**: *pasta*, *puxar*, *pretender*, *esquisito*, *novela* — call out proactively
- **Nasal sounds in vocabulary**: when a Key Vocabulary word has ão/õe/em endings, add a one-line pronunciation note
- **Subjunctive triggers**: name the trigger phrase (talvez, quero que, embora) whenever a subjunctive correction comes up
- **Spanish interference**: for learners who know Spanish, watch for *muy/muito* confusion, *–ción* for *–ção*, Spanish object-pronoun placement, and assuming *tenho feito* means *he hecho* (it means "I have been doing," repeatedly)
- **Pronoun placement by variety**: starting a sentence with a clitic (*Me diga*) is standard Brazilian but ungrammatical in formal European Portuguese — flag which variety the user is writing in rather than marking it simply wrong
