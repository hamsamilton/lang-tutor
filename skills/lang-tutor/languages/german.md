# German Tutor Guide

Read this together with `_common.md`, which defines the feedback block formats, the universal deep-dive types, and the baseline level tables. This file supplies what is specific to German.

## Conventions

Default to standard German (Hochdeutsch). Note du/Sie register on translations where the choice matters. Always teach nouns **with** their article — a bare noun is a half-learned noun.

## Framework Alignment: CEFR / Goethe-Institut

German certification maps one-to-one onto CEFR through the Goethe-Institut exams (also ÖSD in Austria, telc for professional contexts):

| Guide level | CEFR | Goethe exam | Can-do target |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beginner | A1-A2 | Goethe A1 (Start Deutsch 1), A2 | Describe self, family, and routine; handle predictable transactions; simple past narration with Perfekt |
| Intermediate | B1-B2 | Goethe B1 (Zertifikat), B2 | Narrate and argue; handle work, study, and travel; manage subordinate clauses and full case system |
| Advanced | C1-C2 | Goethe C1, C2 (Großes Deutsches Sprachdiplom) | Academic and professional register; Konjunktiv, nominal style, particle nuance |

## Grammar Syllabus

The standard Goethe/CEFR sequence. Within each level, earlier items are foundational and should be taught before later ones. This drives both the Grammar Corrections priority in Mode 1 and the Concept Spotlight in Mode 2.

- **Beginner (A1-A2)**: gender & articles → present tense → **verb-second (V2) word order** → accusative case → sein/haben → modal verbs → separable verbs and prefix placement → Perfekt with haben vs. sein → dative case → possessives → imperative
- **Intermediate (B1-B2)**: two-way prepositions (Wechselpräpositionen — accusative for motion, dative for location) → **adjective endings** (strong / weak / mixed declension) → subordinate clauses with verb-final order (weil, dass, wenn, obwohl) → relative clauses → Präteritum for writing → passive voice → Konjunktiv II for politeness and hypotheticals → genitive → n-declension nouns
- **Advanced (C1-C2)**: Konjunktiv I for reported speech → extended participial attributes (*der gestern angekommene Brief*) → nominal style (Nominalisierung) typical of academic German → **modal particles** (doch, mal, ja, eben, halt) → Funktionsverbgefüge (*in Betracht ziehen*) → register from colloquial to formal

## Irregulars to Prioritize

Feeds the Irregularity Watch in `_common.md`. German irregularity is concentrated in the strong verbs, and it is far more patterned than it first appears — the ablaut classes are inherited from Proto-Germanic and are the *same* classes that produce English *sing/sang/sung*.

**Class irregular** — teach the class, not the word:
- **Ablaut classes** for strong verbs, each with a fixed vowel sequence:

  | Pattern | Infinitive → Präteritum → Partizip II | Also |
  |---|---|---|
  | ei – ie – ie | bleiben → blieb → geblieben | schreiben, steigen, treiben |
  | i – a – u | singen → sang → gesungen | trinken, finden, binden |
  | e – a – o | nehmen → nahm → genommen | sprechen, brechen, helfen |
  | a – u – a | fahren → fuhr → gefahren | tragen, schlagen, waschen |

  Point out the English cognate chain where it exists (*singen/sang/gesungen* ↔ *sing/sang/sung*) — it makes the class memorable rather than arbitrary.
- **Mixed verbs** take a strong stem change with weak endings: bringen → brachte → gebracht; denken → dachte; kennen → kannte; wissen → wusste. A small closed group.
- **Gender by suffix** is partly predictable, which learners are rarely told: **-ung, -heit, -keit, -schaft, -ion, -tät** → *die*; **-chen, -lein, -ment, -um** → *das*; **-er, -ling, -ismus** → *der*. Give the rule whenever a matching noun appears.

**Locally irregular** — name where it's safe:
- Many strong verbs change their vowel in the **2nd and 3rd person singular present only**: fahren → du fährst, er fährt (but *ich fahre, wir fahren* are regular); geben → du gibst; laufen → du läufst; nehmen → du nimmst. Four of six forms are perfectly regular — say so.
- **Modal verbs** are irregular only in the singular present (ich kann, du kannst, er kann — no -e, no -t) and otherwise behave normally.

**Fully irregular** — must be memorized:
- **sein** — suppletive throughout (ist / war / gewesen), the most irregular verb in the language; also *haben* and *werden*
- **Plural formation**: German has five plural patterns (-e, -er, -(e)n, -s, no change), several with umlaut, and **no reliable rule**. Learn every noun as a triple — article + singular + plural (*das Buch, die Bücher*). This is the single most under-taught irregularity in German.
- **N-declension nouns** (die schwache Deklination): a group of masculine nouns taking -n in every case but the nominative singular — *der Junge → den Jungen*, *der Student → dem Studenten*, *der Name*, *der Herr*. Learners never guess these.
- Irregular comparatives: *gut → besser → best-*, *viel → mehr → meist-*, *gern → lieber → liebst-*, *hoch → höher*, *nah → näher*

**By level**: at A1-A2 prioritize *sein/haben/werden*, the modal verbs, and plural forms with every new noun; at B1-B2 the ablaut classes and n-declension; at C1-C2 Konjunktiv I forms and irregular participial attributes.

## Error Categories to Watch For

In Mode 1 Grammar Corrections, prioritize: case errors (nominative/accusative/dative/genitive, especially after prepositions), word order (verb-second in main clauses, verb-final in subordinate clauses), separable verbs (prefix placement), noun gender and adjective endings, haben vs. sein in perfect tense.

Flag du/Sie mismatches when context makes one clearly right.

In Vocabulary Translations, always give the gender, and flag false friends proactively: *bekommen* (to receive, not become), *aktuell* (current), *eventuell* (possibly), *Gift* (poison), *also* (so/therefore), *Rat* (advice).

## Additional Deep-Dive Types

These join the rotation alongside Concept Spotlight and Word Family from `_common.md`.

- **Conjugation Table**: Use the table format and level scaling defined in `_common.md`, with these German specifics:
  - **Persons**: full 6 (ich / du / er-sie-es / wir / ihr / sie-Sie). Note that *wir*, *sie*, and *Sie* always share a form.
  - **Beginner (A1-A2)**: present tense, marking the 2sg/3sg vowel change where it occurs; then Perfekt showing haben vs. sein
  - **Intermediate (B1-B2)**: present / Präteritum / Perfekt side by side (the three-principal-parts view German textbooks use); then Konjunktiv II
  - **Advanced (C1-C2)**: Konjunktiv I vs. II vs. indicative for reported speech
  - **Pattern line**: name the class (weak, strong with its ablaut pattern, mixed, or modal) and one more verb in it

## Watch-outs

- **Case after prepositions**: mit/aus/zu take dative, für/ohne/um take accusative, two-way prepositions split — name the rule on every case correction
- **Verb position**: verb-second in main clauses, verb-final after weil/dass/wenn — the top structural error for English speakers
- **Separable verbs**: the prefix goes to the end (ich rufe dich an) — flag every miss
- **Noun gender**: always teach nouns WITH their article; a bare noun is a half-learned noun
- **False friends**: *bekommen*, *aktuell*, *eventuell*, *Gift*, *also* — call out proactively
- **Plural forms**: never introduce a noun without its plural — there is no rule, and a noun learned without it will be pluralized wrongly
- **Adjective endings**: the ending depends on the article type (der/ein/none), not just case and gender — when correcting, name which of the three declensions applies
- **Umlaut in comparatives**: many one-syllable adjectives take umlaut (*alt → älter*, *groß → größer*, *jung → jünger*) while others don't — flag membership when one appears
