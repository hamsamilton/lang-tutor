# Spanish Tutor Guide

Read this together with `_common.md`, which defines the feedback block formats, the universal deep-dive types, and the baseline level tables. This file supplies what is specific to Spanish.

## Conventions

Default to Latin American Spanish; mention Spain (vosotros, distinción) variants only when relevant or when the user prefers them.

## Framework Alignment: CEFR / DELE

Spanish proficiency is standardized by the Instituto Cervantes, whose **Plan Curricular** sequences grammar directly against CEFR bands; the DELE exams certify at those same six levels:

| Guide level | CEFR / DELE | Can-do target |
|---|---|---|
| Beginner | A1-A2 | Describe self, family, and routine; handle predictable transactions and simple past narration |
| Intermediate | B1-B2 | Narrate and argue in past and future; express wishes, doubt, and emotion; handle work and study topics with fluency |
| Advanced | C1-C2 | Full mood and tense control; nuanced register; comfortable with regional variation, idiom, and implicit meaning |

## Grammar Syllabus

The Plan Curricular sequence. Within each level, earlier items are foundational and should be taught before later ones. This drives both the Grammar Corrections priority in Mode 1 and the Concept Spotlight in Mode 2.

- **Beginner (A1-A2)**: ser vs. estar → gender & number agreement → present indicative (regular, then stem-changing) → definite/indefinite articles → gustar-type verbs (inverted subject) → ir a + infinitive → preterite (regular) → imperfect → reflexive verbs
- **Intermediate (B1-B2)**: preterite vs. imperfect contrast → object pronouns (direct, indirect, combined, placement) → por vs. para → present perfect & pluperfect → future & conditional → **present subjunctive + its triggers** (querer que, es importante que, ojalá, cuando + future) → imperfect subjunctive → si clauses (real and hypothetical) → passive and impersonal *se*
- **Advanced (C1-C2)**: subjunctive across all tenses and sequence-of-tenses rules → mixed and inverted si clauses → verbal periphrases (llevar + gerund, acabar de, ponerse a, volver a) → discourse markers (no obstante, por consiguiente) → register and regional variation (voseo, leísmo, Peninsular vs. American preterite use) → literary and formal constructions

Add this row to both level tables in `_common.md`:

| Aspect | Beginner (A1-A2) | Intermediate (B1-B2) | Advanced (C1-C2) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grammar focus (CEFR) | ser/estar, agreement, present indicative, gustar, ir a + inf, preterite, imperfect | preterite vs. imperfect, object pronouns, por/para, present & imperfect subjunctive, si clauses | subjunctive sequence, mixed si clauses, periphrases, discourse markers, regional register |

## Error Categories to Watch For

In Mode 1 Grammar Corrections, prioritize: ser vs. estar, preterite vs. imperfect, missed subjunctive triggers (quiero que, es importante que, cuando + future), gender/number agreement, por vs. para, object pronoun placement.

In Vocabulary Translations, flag false friends proactively: *embarazada* (pregnant, not embarrassed), *actual* (current), *realizar* (to carry out), *asistir* (to attend), *éxito* (success), *constipado* (having a cold).

## Irregulars to Prioritize

Feeds the Irregularity Watch in `_common.md`. Spanish irregularity is unusually systematic — most of it is class irregularity wearing a scary costume, so name the class rather than treating each verb as a special case.

**Class irregular** — teach the class, not the word:
- **Stem-changers**: e→ie (*pensar, querer, sentir*), o→ue (*poder, dormir, volver*), e→i (*pedir, servir*). The change hits the stressed syllable only, so *nosotros* stays regular — say this, it removes half the fear.
- **Yo-go verbs**: *tengo, pongo, salgo, vengo, hago, digo* — irregular in the 1sg present only, which then seeds the whole present subjunctive
- **Preterite strong stems**: *estuv-, tuv-, pud-, pus-, quis-, hic-, vin-, dij-* — all take the same unstressed ending set (-e, -iste, -o, -imos, -ieron), so one pattern unlocks the group
- **Spelling-change verbs**: -car → -qu-, -gar → -gu-, -zar → -c- before *e* (*buscar → busqué*). Purely orthographic — the sound doesn't change, so reassure rather than drill.

**Locally irregular** — name where it's safe:
- Most Spanish verbs are irregular in *some* tenses only. The **imperfect is almost entirely regular** — only *ser (era)*, *ir (iba)*, and *ver (veía)* are irregular. Tell learners this; it is a genuine relief.

**Fully irregular** — must be memorized:
- *ser*, *ir*, *haber*, and the shared preterite **fui/fuiste/fue** that serves *both* ser and ir (suppletive — disambiguated only by context)
- Irregular past participles: *hecho, dicho, visto, puesto, escrito, vuelto, muerto, roto, abierto*
- Irregular comparatives: *bueno → mejor*, *malo → peor*, *grande → mayor*
- Irregular affirmative tú commands: *di, haz, ve, pon, sal, ten, ven, sé*

**By level**: at A1-A2 prioritize *ser/estar/ir/tener/haber* and the stem-changing classes; at B1-B2 the preterite strong stems and irregular participles; at C1-C2 subjunctive irregulars and defective verbs.

## Additional Deep-Dive Types

These join the rotation alongside Concept Spotlight and Word Family from `_common.md`.

- **False Friend Alert**: If the translation contains a word that looks like an English word but means something different, break down the difference with one example of each.

- **Conjugation Table**: Use the table format and level scaling defined in `_common.md`, with these Spanish specifics:
  - **Persons**: use the 5-form Latin American paradigm (yo / tú / él-ella-usted / nosotros / ellos-ustedes) — omit *vosotros* unless the user is learning Peninsular Spanish. Mention *vos* only when regional variation is the point.
  - **Beginner (A1-A2)**: present indicative first; then preterite, then imperfect once those are introduced
  - **Intermediate (B1-B2)**: preterite vs. imperfect side by side (the single most useful contrast table in Spanish); later present indicative vs. present subjunctive
  - **Advanced (C1-C2)**: indicative / subjunctive / conditional across moods, or the full subjunctive sequence (present vs. imperfect subjunctive) for si-clause work
  - **Pattern line**: name whether the verb is regular, stem-changing (e→ie *pensar*, o→ue *poder*, e→i *pedir*), or irregular, and give one verb from the same class

- **Regional Variation**: Take one phrase from the translation and show how it differs across the Spanish-speaking world (tú/vos/usted, Peninsular vs. American vocabulary or tense preference), with a one-line note on where each is used. Favor this at advanced level, where CEFR treats sociolinguistic range as a C-level competence.

For the Spanish **Word Family** deep-dive specifically, favor verb/noun/adjective derivations (*trabajar → trabajo → trabajador*) or English cognate chains with their sound-correspondence rule (-ción → -tion, -dad → -ty).

## Watch-outs

- **Ser vs. estar**: the most persistent English-speaker error — correct every miss with the one-line reason (essence vs. state)
- **Subjunctive blindness**: English speakers don't see the triggers; name the trigger phrase whenever a subjunctive correction comes up
- **Preterite vs. imperfect**: correct with the narrative framing (completed event vs. background/ongoing)
- **False friends**: *embarazada*, *actual*, *éxito*, *realizar*, *constipado* — call them out proactively when nearby words appear
- **Gender agreement chains**: when one noun's gender is wrong, every adjective downstream breaks — fix the chain, not just the noun
- **Gustar-type inversion**: "yo gusto" for "I like" is a structural error, not a slip — re-explain that the thing liked is the subject (*me gusta el libro* = "the book pleases me")
- **Object pronoun placement**: before a conjugated verb but attached to infinitives/gerunds/affirmative commands (*lo quiero ver* / *quiero verlo*) — English speakers default to English order
- **Overusing subject pronouns**: *yo* and *tú* in every sentence is unnatural when the conjugation already marks person — show the dropped version
