# Bengali Tutor Guide

Read this together with `_common.md`, which defines the feedback block formats, the universal deep-dive types, and the baseline level tables. This file supplies what is specific to Bengali.

## Conventions

Bengali (Bangla) uses its own script, is Indo-Aryan, and follows SOV word order. Always include transliteration alongside Bangla text at beginner and intermediate levels.

## Framework Alignment: a fragmented-standards caveat

Bengali has **no single dominant proficiency framework** for foreign learners, and it is worth saying so rather than implying one. In use:

- **CEFR**, adapted by universities teaching Bangla as a foreign language
- **ACTFL**, in North American South Asian studies and heritage programs
- Domestic curricula from the Bangla Academy (Bangladesh) and West Bengal's education board, both aimed at native speakers

| Guide level | ≈ CEFR | ≈ ACTFL | Can-do target |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beginner | A1-A2 | Novice–Intermediate Low | Script; SOV; postpositions; classifiers; present and past; the three registers |
| Intermediate | B1-B2 | Intermediate Mid–Advanced Low | -কে object marker; compound verbs; conjunctive participles; perfect tenses |
| Advanced | C1-C2 | Advanced Mid–Superior | Sadhu vs. Cholit register; presumptive and conditional moods; literary style |

## Grammar Syllabus

The standard sequence. Within each level, earlier items are foundational and should be taught before later ones. This drives both the Grammar Corrections priority in Mode 1 and the Concept Spotlight in Mode 2.

- **Beginner (A1-A2)**: the Bangla script → **no grammatical gender at all** → SOV word order → **postpositions** rather than prepositions → **classifiers for counting** (টা for things, জন for people, খানা for flat objects) → the copula and its absence in the present → present simple and present continuous → **the three honorific registers তুই / তুমি / আপনি** and their distinct verb endings → simple past
- **Intermediate (B1-B2)**: **the -কে object marker** for definite and animate objects → **compound verbs** (নিয়ে যাওয়া, করে ফেলা as aspect-and-nuance markers) → **the conjunctive participle** (করে, গিয়ে — chaining clauses, extremely common and hard for English speakers to produce) → present perfect and past perfect → future → conditional → relative-correlative clauses (যে… সে)
- **Advanced (C1-C2)**: **Sadhu bhasha (literary) vs. Cholit bhasha (colloquial)** — two full registers with different verb and pronoun forms → presumptive mood → participial constructions → passive → literary and journalistic style → idiom

## Irregulars to Prioritize

Feeds the Irregularity Watch in `_common.md`. Open with the good news: **Bengali has no grammatical gender.** Nouns, adjectives, and verbs never inflect for it — a real simplification against Hindi, and worth stating to any learner arriving from there. The irregularity that remains is concentrated in verb stem vowels.

**Class irregular** — teach the class, not the word:
- **Vowel mutation in verb stems** is the central pattern. The stem vowel raises in some person forms and not others, entirely predictably once the class is named: লেখা *lekha* (write) → লিখি *likhi* (I write) but লেখেন *lekhen* (you-formal write); দেখা *dækha* (see) → দেখি *dekhi*. Verbs sort into a small number of vowel classes (a/e, e/i, o/u), and naming the class explains the whole paradigm rather than one form.
- **The three honorific registers are a regular system**, not three vocabularies: তুই / তুমি / আপনি each take a consistent ending set across every verb (যাস / যাও / যান for "go"). Teach the ending set once and it applies universally.
- **Classifier choice** groups by noun type — টা general, জন people, খানা flat objects, গাছা long thin objects — with টা as the safe default when unsure. Say that; it gives the learner a fallback.

**Locally irregular** — name where it's safe:
- Bengali verbs conjugate for **person and register but never for gender or number** — so the paradigm is smaller than it first appears, and a form learned for one subject works for singular and plural alike.
- Stem mutation fires only in **specific person forms**. The stem is stable elsewhere, so tell the learner which slots to watch rather than treating the verb as unpredictable.

**Fully irregular** — must be memorized:
- **যাওয়া** (to go) is suppletive — the past tense switches to a completely different stem: যাই *jai* (I go) but গেলাম *gelam* (I went). The highest-frequency irregular in the language.
- Other irregular high-frequency verbs: হওয়া (to be/become), খাওয়া (to eat), দেওয়া (to give), নেওয়া (to take), পাওয়া (to get) — the -ওয়া verbs contract unpredictably
- **The two registers use different verb forms outright**: Sadhu *করিয়াছি* against Cholit *করেছি* for "I have done." A learner reading older literature meets an apparently different language — name the register rather than correcting it as archaic error.
- **Spelling versus pronunciation**: Bangla orthography preserves Sanskrit forms the pronunciation has abandoned — the inherent vowel is often silent or realized as *o*, and conjunct consonants can be opaque (জ্ঞান is pronounced *gyan*, not *jnan*). Flag these as reading facts, never as spelling errors.

**By level**: at A1-A2 prioritize the classifier for each new noun, the register ending sets, and যাওয়া/হওয়া; at B1-B2 the stem-vowel classes and the -ওয়া verbs; at C1-C2 the Sadhu forms and conjunct-consonant readings.

## Error Categories to Watch For

In Mode 1 Grammar Corrections, prioritize: SOV word order, postpositions rather than prepositions, verb conjugation marking both tense **and** honorific register (তুই/তুমি/আপনি each take different endings — a sharper three-way split than Hindi's two-tier system), classifiers when counting, and the -কে object marker for definite or animate direct objects.

Note that English loanwords are extremely common in spoken Bengali, especially in urban speech — distinguish "this is fine in everyday Bangla" from "here's the pure Bengali word" rather than correcting reflexively.

## Watch-outs

- **Three-tier honorific register**: তুই / তুমি / আপনি each take different verb endings (যাস / যাও / যান) — using তুই with a stranger or elder reads as rude, and this is a sharper distinction than Hindi's two-tier system, so flag register mismatches explicitly
- **Classifiers are mandatory when counting**: দুইটা বই, not দুই বই — টা (general), জন (people), or খানা (flat objects) cannot be dropped
- **No grammatical gender**: learners coming from Hindi sometimes over-apply gender agreement out of habit — reassure rather than merely correct
- **Object marker -কে**: definite or animate direct objects take -কে, indefinite or inanimate ones usually don't — English speakers frequently omit it
- **Verb-final order with dropped pronouns**: subject pronouns are often omitted once the conjugation establishes them (যাচ্ছি "[I] am going") — this can feel incomplete to English speakers expecting an explicit subject
- **The conjunctive participle**: Bengali chains clauses with করে / গিয়ে where English uses "and" or a subordinate clause — learners rarely produce it unprompted, so offer it as naturalness feedback when a string of short sentences would read better joined
- **Script vs. sound**: never flag a learner's transliteration for matching pronunciation rather than spelling — note the standard written form and move on
