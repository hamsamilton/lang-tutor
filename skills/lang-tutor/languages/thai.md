# Thai Tutor Guide

Read this together with `_common.md`, which defines the feedback block formats, the universal deep-dive types, and the baseline level tables. This file supplies what is specific to Thai.

## Conventions

Thai has no single standard romanization — default to the Royal Thai General System (RTGS) for all transliterations, and say so if the user asks why a word looks different elsewhere. Thai script has no spaces between words within a sentence (spaces mark clause/sentence boundaries instead), so don't expect or introduce word-spacing when showing Thai text.

## Framework Alignment: CU-TFL / CEFR

Thai proficiency is most commonly certified by **CU-TFL** (Chulalongkorn University's Test of Thai as a Foreign Language), which reports on an ACTFL-style scale, alongside CEFR-adapted curricula used by other institutions:

| Guide level | ≈ CEFR | ≈ CU-TFL / ACTFL | Can-do target |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beginner | A1-A2 | Novice–Intermediate Low | Five tones; script and tone rules; classifiers; politeness particles; aspect markers |
| Intermediate | B1-B2 | Intermediate Mid–Advanced Low | Question particles; serial verbs; complements; passive; relative clauses |
| Advanced | C1-C2 | Advanced Mid–Superior | Register tiers including formal and royal vocabulary; sentence-final particles; literary style |

## Grammar Syllabus

The standard sequence. Within each level, earlier items are foundational and should be taught before later ones. This drives both the Grammar Corrections priority in Mode 1 and the Concept Spotlight in Mode 2.

- **Beginner (A1-A2)**: the five tones → **the script and its tone rules** (see below) → topic-comment structure → **classifiers** for counting → **politeness particles** ครับ / ค่ะ-คะ → **no tense conjugation** — time words and aspect markers instead (แล้ว / กำลัง / จะ) → negation ไม่ → basic questions with ไหม
- **Intermediate (B1-B2)**: question particles (ไหม / หรือเปล่า / ใช่ไหม) and their differing force → **serial verb constructions** → result and direction complements (ไป / มา / ได้ / ออก) → passive with ถูก / โดน (usually unfavorable) → relative clauses with ที่ → comparison with กว่า / ที่สุด → conjunctions
- **Advanced (C1-C2)**: **the register tiers** — everyday, polite, formal, monastic, and royal vocabulary → sentence-final particles for nuance (นะ / สิ / ล่ะ / หรอก) → the Pali-Sanskrit vocabulary layer → literary and journalistic style → regional and colloquial variation

## Irregulars to Prioritize

Feeds the Irregularity Watch in `_common.md`. Thai is **isolating** — words never change form, so there is no conjugation or agreement to be irregular. Say this; it is a large simplification. The difficulty sits entirely in the **writing system** and in a few arbitrary pairings.

**Class irregular** — teach the class, not the word:
- **The tone rules are the real system to learn.** A syllable's tone is not written directly — it is computed from four inputs: the **consonant class** (high, mid, or low — all 44 consonants belong to one), the **tone mark** (or absence of one), the **syllable type** (live or dead), and the **vowel length**. The same tone mark produces different tones on different consonant classes. This is the steepest orthography-to-sound mapping of any major language, and it is fully regular — so teach the rule, never the individual word's tone. When correcting a tone, name which input the learner got wrong.
- **Silent letters marked by ์** (การันต์): Sanskrit and Pali loans keep their original spelling with the unpronounced parts marked — ประโยชน์ *prayot*, สงสาร, จันทร์ *chan*. Regular once the marker is recognized.
- **The Pali-Sanskrit layer** behaves like Sino-Vietnamese or Latinate English: bound forms that appear in compounds and carry a formal register. Naming the layer explains why a word looks long and why it can't stand alone.

**Locally irregular** — name where it's safe:
- **Aspect markers are optional.** แล้ว / กำลัง / จะ appear only when the time isn't already clear from context or a time word. Learners over-apply them from English habit — telling them the markers are droppable is correct and freeing.
- Once the tone rules are internalized, **Thai spelling is highly predictable** in the native vocabulary. The exceptions cluster in the Pali-Sanskrit borrowings, so the learner can be told where the irregularity lives rather than fearing all of it.

**Fully irregular** — must be memorized:
- **Classifier-noun pairings** are arbitrary and per-noun: คน for people, ตัว for animals, furniture, and clothing, ใบ for round or hollow objects, เล่ม for books, คัน for vehicles, ลูก for fruit and balls, องค์ for monks and royalty. Always give the classifier with a new noun — a noun learned without it cannot be counted.
- **Register-tiered vocabulary** — the same everyday concept has several words, and the choice is socially, not stylistically, determined. "To eat" alone gives: กิน (plain), ทาน (polite), รับประทาน (formal), ฉัน (of monks), เสวย (of royalty). Similar tiers exist for "to speak," "to go," and "to give." Flag which tier a word belongs to whenever one appears.
- **Irregular spellings in loanwords** where the written form preserves an etymology the pronunciation has abandoned
- **Tone minimal pairs** in high-frequency vocabulary: ขาว *khǎao* (white) / ข้าว *khâao* (rice) / เขา *khǎo* (he-she); ไหม *mǎi* (silk) / ไม่ *mâi* (not) / ไม้ *máai* (wood); สวย *sǔuay* (beautiful) / ซวย *suay* (unlucky)

**By level**: at A1-A2 prioritize the tone rules and the classifier of every new noun; at B1-B2 the ถูก/โดน connotation and serial-verb patterns; at C1-C2 the register tiers and Pali-Sanskrit vocabulary.

## Error Categories to Watch For

In Mode 1 Grammar Corrections, prioritize: wrong tone changing the word entirely, missing or wrong classifiers when counting, topic-comment structure instead of English SVO framing, attempts to conjugate for tense rather than using time words and aspect markers, dropping or misusing politeness particles ครับ / ค่ะ-คะ, and unnatural serial verb constructions.

## Watch-outs

- **Tone minimal pairs**: a single wrong tone produces a different word entirely (สวย *sǔuay* "beautiful" vs. ส่วย *sùuay* "tribute"; ไหม *mǎi* "silk" vs. ไม่ *mâi* "not" vs. ไม้ *máai* "wood") — always flag these explicitly, don't let them slide as pronunciation quirks
- **Classifiers are mandatory when counting**: หนังสือ 3 เล่ม, not หนังสือ 3 — correct every bare-noun count at beginner level
- **No tense conjugation**: learners try to force English-style tense onto verbs; redirect to time words (เมื่อวาน, พรุ่งนี้) and aspect markers (แล้ว/กำลัง/จะ)
- **Politeness particles aren't optional filler**: ครับ/ค่ะ mark both politeness and the speaker's gender — a male speaker using ค่ะ, or a female speaker dropping ค่ะ in polite contexts, reads as very odd
- **No spaces within sentences**: when showing Thai script, don't insert spaces between words — spaces mark clause or sentence breaks, not word boundaries
- **Tone rules over tone memorization**: when correcting a tone, name the consonant class and syllable type that determine it — otherwise the learner memorizes one word instead of learning the system
- **Register tier mismatch**: using กิน where รับประทาน is expected is a social error, not a grammatical one — flag it as register, and say who the audience would be for each
