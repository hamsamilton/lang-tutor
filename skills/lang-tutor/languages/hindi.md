# Hindi Tutor Guide

Read this together with `_common.md`, which defines the feedback block formats, the universal deep-dive types, and the baseline level tables. This file supplies what is specific to Hindi.

## Conventions

Always include transliteration alongside Devanagari at beginner and intermediate levels. Default to the polite आप register in translations unless context calls for informal तुम.

## Framework Alignment: a fragmented-standards caveat

Hindi has **no single dominant proficiency framework**, and it is worth saying so rather than implying one. The frameworks actually in use:

- **CEFR**, adapted by European and increasingly Indian institutions
- **ACTFL** (Novice / Intermediate / Advanced / Superior) in North American university programs
- The **Kendriya Hindi Sansthan** and Dakshina Bharat Hindi Prachar Sabha ladders (Parichay → Prathmik → Madhyama → Rashtrabhasha → Praveen → Pragya), widely used within India

| Guide level | ≈ CEFR | ≈ ACTFL | Can-do target |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beginner | A1-A2 | Novice–Intermediate Low | Devanagari; gender agreement; SOV; postpositions; present habitual and simple past |
| Intermediate | B1-B2 | Intermediate Mid–Advanced Low | **Ergative ने**; oblique case; compound verbs; subjunctive and future |
| Advanced | C1-C2 | Advanced Mid–Superior | Presumptive mood; Sanskritic vs. Persianate register; relative-correlative clauses; literary style |

A useful practical note: **spoken Hindi and Urdu are largely the same language** at the everyday register, diverging mainly in script and in high-register vocabulary. If the user's goal is conversation, say so; it doubles the material available to them.

## Grammar Syllabus

The standard teaching sequence across these frameworks. Within each level, earlier items are foundational and should be taught before later ones. This drives both the Grammar Corrections priority in Mode 1 and the Concept Spotlight in Mode 2.

- **Beginner (A1-A2)**: Devanagari → two genders (masculine/feminine) → **SOV word order** → the copula है/हैं → **postpositions rather than prepositions** → adjective agreement → present habitual (करता है) → the three "you"s (तू / तुम / आप) and their distinct verb forms → **oblique case before postpositions** → simple past
- **Intermediate (B1-B2)**: **the ergative ने construction** → perfective aspect and its agreement rules → continuous aspect (कर रहा है) → future → subjunctive → **compound verbs** (जाना / लेना / देना as aspect-and-nuance markers) → causatives (करना → कराना) → relative-correlative clauses (जो… वो)
- **Advanced (C1-C2)**: presumptive mood (होगा) → **Sanskritic (tatsama) vs. Persianate register** and when each is appropriate → participial constructions → passive → literary and journalistic style → idiom and proverb

## Irregulars to Prioritize

Feeds the Irregularity Watch in `_common.md`. Hindi verb endings are remarkably regular; the difficulty sits in **noun gender**, the **oblique case**, and a short list of irregular perfectives.

**Class irregular** — teach the class, not the word:
- **The oblique case** is regular but invisible to learners: masculine nouns ending in -आ shift to -ए before a postposition (लड़का → लड़के को), and all plurals take -ओं (लड़कों को, किताबों में). Nouns not ending in -आ don't change in the singular. Name the class rather than correcting the single form.
- **Causative pairs** follow a recurring shape: करना → कराना (have done), देखना → दिखाना (show), सुनना → सुनाना (tell), सीखना → सिखाना (teach). One pattern, many verbs.
- **Gender by ending** is partly predictable and learners are rarely told: most nouns ending in **-आ** are masculine (कमरा, लड़का) and most ending in **-ई** are feminine (लड़की, कुर्सी); Sanskrit-derived **-ता / -ति** nouns are feminine. Give the rule whenever a matching noun appears — but see below for how often it fails.

**Locally irregular** — name where it's safe:
- Irregular Hindi verbs are irregular **only in the perfective**. Their present, continuous, future, and subjunctive forms are perfectly regular. Say this — it confines the problem to one slot.
- The **ergative ने appears only in the perfective of transitive verbs**. In every other tense the verb agrees with the subject as expected. Learners often believe ने is pervasive; telling them it is confined to one aspect makes it tractable.

**Fully irregular** — must be memorized:
- **Irregular perfectives** — a short, high-frequency list: करना → किया, जाना → गया, होना → हुआ, देना → दिया, लेना → लिया, पीना → पिया. These are among the most common verbs in the language, so they cannot be deferred.
- **होना** is suppletive across tenses: है / था / होगा / हो / हुआ
- **Gender of inanimate nouns is arbitrary** where the ending rule fails, and it fails often: किताब (feminine, consonant-final), मकान (masculine), चीज़ (feminine), पानी (masculine despite -ई), दही (masculine). Since the verb *and* every adjective agree with gender, one wrong guess cascades through the whole sentence. Always teach nouns with gender.
- **Ergative agreement**: in the perfective with ने, the verb agrees with the **object**, not the subject — मैंने किताब पढ़ी (feminine, agreeing with किताब). If the object also carries को, the verb defaults to masculine singular. Structurally alien to English and worth explaining every time.

**By level**: at A1-A2 prioritize the gender of every new noun and the oblique -ए shift; at B1-B2 the irregular perfectives and ergative agreement; at C1-C2 register-paired synonyms across the Sanskritic/Persianate layers.

## Error Categories to Watch For

In Mode 1 Grammar Corrections, prioritize: gender agreement (verbs and adjectives agree with noun gender), the ergative ने with transitive past-tense verbs, postposition choice (में/पर/को/से), SOV word order, honorific consistency (तू/तुम/आप each take different verb forms), and oblique-case shifts.

Note that English loanwords are often genuinely natural in spoken Hindi — distinguish "this is fine in Hinglish" from "here's the pure Hindi word" rather than correcting reflexively.

## Watch-outs

- **Ergative ने**: in the perfective with transitive verbs, the verb agrees with the *object*, not the subject (मैंने किताब पढ़ी) — the single most confusing rule; explain it on every correction
- **Gender of inanimate nouns**: किताब is feminine, मकान is masculine — there's no logic to memorize around, so always teach nouns with gender
- **Honorific agreement**: आप takes plural verb forms even for one person — flag singular-verb slips
- **Oblique case**: nouns change form before postpositions (लड़का → लड़के को) — point out the shift when correcting
- **Compound verbs**: bare जाना vs. चला जाना changes nuance (completion/finality) — introduce gradually rather than flagging as errors
- **Register layering**: the Sanskritic and Persianate vocabularies carry real social meaning; when suggesting a synonym, say which layer it belongs to rather than presenting it as neutral
